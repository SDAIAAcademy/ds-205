Before any data

A test can be wrong in two ways, and you choose both rates in advance

The test says "effect"The test says "no clear effect"
The reminder does nothingFalse win. You accept 5% of these: the level, α.Correct.
The reminder adds 2 pointsCorrect. The chance of landing here is the power: 80% by convention.Missed effect. 20% of the time at 80% power.
Both rates are prices. A false win costs the office a reminder that does not pay. A missed effect costs it a reminder that would have.

DS-201 taught the p-value and the 5% level. Power is the new idea today. The conventions, 5% and 80%, are conventions: a team that fears a missed effect more can ask for 90%, and the lab's stretch prices that at a third more holders.

Two proportions, two arms

Three things drive the sample size, and one of them is squared

def n_per_arm(p1, delta,
              alpha=0.05, power=0.80):
    p2 = p1 + delta
    za = stats.norm.ppf(1 - alpha / 2)  # 1.96
    zb = stats.norm.ppf(power)          # 0.84
    spread = p1 * (1 - p1) + p2 * (1 - p2)
    return ceil((za + zb) ** 2
                * spread / delta ** 2)
  • The error rates you accept: 1.96 and 0.84.
  • The spread of a yes-or-no outcome, largest at 50%.
  • The minimum effect, squared, in the denominator.
  • Only the third is open to discussion with the director.

Participants write this function themselves in lab 2.1, step 2, without an assistant. Do not derive it. Say what each line is for: how sure you want to be, how noisy the outcome is, how small a difference you must see. The function returns units per arm, and the unit is whatever was randomised.

02
The DS-201 habit, before the test

To check a design, run it a thousand times in a world you built

1 · Build a worldbaseline 61.58%; the reminder truly adds 2 points
→
2 · Run the plan9,187 holders per arm, the pre-registered test
→
3 · Repeat1,000 times, with a seed
→
4 · Countthe share of tests that say "significant"
That share is the power. If the formula is right, it should be about 80%.

In DS-201 participants counted how often an interval contained a known truth. This is the same move: a claim about a method is checked by using the method many times where the truth is known. The difference is that the test has not happened yet. You are rehearsing it.

Lab 2.2, steps 1 and 2

One simulated test is two binomial draws and the planned analysis

def power_sim(gen, n, p1, delta, reps):
    x_c = gen.binomial(n, p1, size=reps)
    x_r = gen.binomial(n, p1 + delta,
                       size=reps)
    _, _, p = U.z_test(x_c, n, x_r, n)
    return (p < 0.05).mean()

power_sim(U.rng(22), 9187,
          0.6158, 0.02, 1000)   # 0.806
  • Each draw is the number of on-time renewals among n holders in one arm.
  • The analysis is the one on the pre-registration, not a shortcut.
  • size=reps runs all the tests at once.
  • The seed makes your 0.806 your neighbour's 0.806.

The second bullet is the reason to simulate: you test the analysis you will really run. If the plan were a t-test on branch means, that is what would go inside the function, and session 3 does exactly that.

Lab 2.2, step 4

The power curve is steep at first and flat at the end

Power to detect 2 points on a baseline of 61.58%.
  • 2,000 per arm: 23.6% simulated. A real effect is missed three times in four.
  • 9,187 per arm: 78.7% on the curve's run, 80% by formula.
  • 30,000 per arm: every simulated test found it.
  • The first 9,000 buy 80 points of power. The next 21,000 buy 20.

Solution run of lab 2.2: 23.6%, 46.8%, 60.5%, 78.7%, 88.4%, 95.2% and 100.0% at 2,000, 4,000, 6,000, 9,187, 12,000, 16,000 and 30,000 per arm. The curve is the evidence the third outcome asks for. A 28-day test will have about 30,000 per arm: it sits on the flat part, where the question is no longer whether there is a 2-point effect but how large the effect is. Such a test detects 1.11 points with 80% power.

What simulation is for

The formula covers one design; a simulation covers the one you have

The formula assumes

Independent units. Two proportions. Equal arms. One look. The baseline you guessed.

Real designs break it

Visitors inside branches. A baseline that turns out to be 50%: power 76.6%, not 80%. An effect that differs by weekday. An analysis on branch means.

The rule. Use the formula for a first number and for the conversation about the minimum effect. Use a simulation of the planned analysis for the number you put on the page.

The 76.6% is the stretch of lab 2.2: same size, true baseline 50%, where a yes-or-no outcome is noisiest. At a 70% baseline the same test has 82.2%. Sources for further reading on sample size in practice: Zhou, Lu and Shallah (2023).

Checkpoint

What does a simulated power of 81% tell you?

You simulate a planned test 1,000 times with a true effect of 2 points. 81% of the simulated tests are significant. Which statement is correct?

There is an 81% chance that the reminder works
If the real test is significant, the effect is real with probability 81%
The reminder will raise on-time renewal by 81% of 2 points
If the effect is really 2 points, a test of this design finds it about 81 times in 100
Why. Power is a statement about the design under an assumed effect: how often it finds what is there.

Answer: D. The two tempting options turn a property of the design into a property of the hypothesis. If the room splits, ask what the 81% would be if the true effect were zero: about 5%.

03
The counter test

Visitors of one branch resemble each other

  • Mansour's test: a triage desk at the entrance. Outcome: served within 15 minutes. Minimum effect: 5 points.
  • Visitors share a queue, so branches are randomised: 12 with the desk, 12 without.
  • Four baseline weeks: 10,888 visits, 62.2% served within 15 minutes.
  • By branch the rate runs from 43.7% to 80.3%.

Intraclass correlation

The share of the outcome's variance that lies between branches. Here 0.033: three per cent of the variation is about which branch you walked into.

Solution run of labs 1.3 and 2.3. The intraclass correlation is usually written ICC. Day 1 settled why the visitor cannot be the unit: interference. Today's question is what that choice costs.

Lab 2.3, step 1

A small correlation times a large branch is a large penalty

0.033

The formula 1 plus (m minus 1) times the intraclass correlation assumes equal cluster sizes; Mashraq's branches run from 202 to 1,011 visits, so treat 15.8 as an approximation and trust the simulation of the next slides. The intuition holds: a correlation that looks negligible is multiplied by the cluster size.

When power is still short

More branches help; more visits per branch hardly do

  • More units: the strongest lever, and here there are no more branches.
  • A longer test: helps a little; the difference between branches stays.
  • Pairing or a baseline: removes the stable part of that difference.
  • A larger minimum effect: an honest answer if the desk is only worth having for a large gain.

And fewer units than you think

Three pairs of branches share staff, so the test has 21 units, not 24. Randomised 10 against 11 without pairing, its power is 20.6%.

Not on the list: a cleverer analysis of the same coin tosses.

The 20.6% is the stretch of lab 2.3. Designs that reuse each unit over time, such as switchbacks, and regression adjustment with pre-period data are the next course's subject: DS-302, days 2 and 3. For this course the honest conclusion for Mansour is: pair the branches, and expect to detect 5 points about six times in ten.

After the last notice

Two more clocks run: the outcome window and the fading of novelty

The outcome window

A notice goes out 30 days before expiry. On-time renewal is known only at the expiry date. The last holders complete the metric 30 days after day 28. A result read earlier misses the holders who renew late in their window.

Novelty

People react to anything new, and the reaction can fade. A short test catches the reaction at its peak. A longer test averages over it and can show the effect week by week.

On the page: the duration, the date on which the primary metric is complete, and a plan to report the effect by week.

Novelty is listed among the common traps of this course and comes back tomorrow as a seeded flaw: a form test stopped on day 8. Attrition, units that drop out of one arm, is the other trap in that family and opens Day 3. Measuring novelty decay properly is part of DS-302.

Three things to keep

The effect is squared

2 points need 9,187 per arm; 1 point needs 36,950. The minimum effect is the director's choice, made from costs.

Simulate the plan

1,000 runs of the planned analysis gave 80.6% power and 5.3% false wins. When the design leaves the formula, only the simulation is left.

Units and calendars

24 branches gave 22.8% power; pairing gave 62.2%. Nine days reach the size; 28 cover the weeks and the month-end.
