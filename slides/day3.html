The reminder test has run its 28 days and the outcome window has closed. Set the scene: a colleague has the platform's export open and is one line of code away from "the effect". Ask the room what they would do first. Most will say "compute the difference". Today starts with why not.

Session 1 checks the assignment before any result is read. Session 2 reads the result as an interval against the minimum effect. Session 3 counts how three ordinary habits manufacture wins. Session 4 turns all of it on someone else's write-up, and ends with the choice of a capstone case.

Review of Day 2

Which plan gives the test a fair chance?

A test needs 9,000 residents per arm for its minimum effect. Traffic is 2,000 residents a day, lower at weekends, with a surge at the month-end. Which plan do you accept?

Run 9 days, until 18,000 residents are in
Run 28 days: whole weeks, with the month-end inside

Why. The formula gives the minimum size. The calendar gives the duration: whole cycles.

Attrition

Units that drop out of one arm after assignment. When the ones that drop differ from the rest, the arms stop being comparable, and the coin's work is undone.

Two different failures produce a bad ratio: a faulty assignment and a loss after assignment. An assignment log written before the test is what tells them apart, which is why the pre-registration asks for a seeded, recorded coin. E-mail holders renew on time less often in general, so removing them from one arm flatters that arm.

What to do

A failed ratio check stops the reading, not necessarily the test

  • Stop reading results. Nothing computed from a broken comparison is worth quoting.
  • Find the cell. Split by segment and by time until the loss is located.
  • Go back to the assignment record. If it is sound and outcomes exist for everyone, analyse as assigned.
  • If the assignment itself was faulty, or outcomes are missing for the lost units, the test cannot be repaired. Fix the cause and run it again.
  • Write it in the result note, whichever it was.

Here the second branch applies: the assignment record is sound and the permit register has an outcome for every holder. Also worth a sentence: a passing ratio check does not prove a sound test. Equal counts can hide a swap of who is in which arm, or outcomes recorded differently by arm. The check catches one family of faults.

Lab 3.2, step 3

"Is there an effect?" and "Is it large enough?" are different questions

The effect as assigned, and what the faulty export would have shown.
  • Is there an effect? Yes. Zero is far outside the interval; p is below one in a million.
  • Is it at least 2 points? The estimate says just. The interval says: between 1.4 and 2.9.
  • The interval straddles the minimum effect.

DS-201 called these statistical and practical significance. A tiny p-value tells the director nothing about the second question, and the second question is the one on her page. Note what the export's interval would have suggested: a result comfortably around 2.7.

The interval against the minimum effect

An interval can sit in four places, and each has its own sentence

Where the interval liesWhat to say
Wholly above the minimum effectThe gain is at least the minimum, beyond reasonable doubt.
Above zero, straddling the minimumThere is a gain. Whether it reaches the minimum is not settled.
Above zero, wholly below the minimumThere is a gain, and it is too small to act on.
Containing zeroThe data are consistent with no effect, and with effects up to the upper limit. Say which.
What goes wrong. All four are reported as "significant" or "not significant", which merges the first three and misdescribes the fourth.

The reminder test is in the second row. The fourth row matters as much: "not significant" with an interval from minus 0.5 to plus 3 does not mean "no effect"; it means the test could not tell, and the upper limit says how large an effect is still possible.

The measuring instrument of this session

In an A/A test nothing differs, so every win is false

  • Both arms get the same thing. The true effect is zero by construction.
  • Run 1,000 of them and count the "significant" ones.
  • With one planned analysis of one metric at the right unit: 5.5% of 1,000.
  • That is the 5% you agreed to. Anything above it was added by a habit.

Three habits, three counts

Looking every day. Looking at twenty metrics. Counting page views in place of applicants. Each feels harmless, and each can be found in reports written by careful people.

Solution run of lab 3.3: 1,000 simulated A/A tests of 28 days with 1,070 holders per arm per day. Teams also run real A/A tests on their platform before trusting it: if the platform itself declares wins at more than 5%, something in the pipeline is wrong. DS-302 returns to that.

Lab 3.3, step 2

A p-value wanders, and 28 looks give it 28 chances to cross 0.05

Three A/A tests that dipped below 0.05 and came back.
  • Rule: stop on the first day the p-value is below 0.05.
  • 27.8% of 1,000 A/A tests declare a win.
  • Half of those false wins are declared by day 5.

Looking is harmless. Acting on the look is the problem: stopping, extending or switching because of what was seen. The early days are the most dangerous: little data, large swings. Source for the mechanism and its remedies: Johari, Pekelis and Walsh, Always Valid Inference.

Two traps that no A/A test shows

Novelty fades and attrition unbalances, even when the analysis is right

Novelty

A first-week reaction to anything new, which can fade. A test of eight days measures the reaction at its peak. Look for the effect by week, and distrust short tests of visible changes.

Attrition

Units that drop out of one arm after assignment: a reminder that never arrived, a form that crashed on some devices. The remaining arms differ in who is left. Look at the sample ratio and at who is missing.

Both change the true comparison, not the false-win rate. They are found by reading the design and the checks, which is the next session.

The catalogue of flaws used in lab 3.4 has ten entries; these two are among the seven seeded in the write-up, as "short run" and "ratio mismatch". Measuring novelty decay properly is in DS-302.

Lab 3.4, steps 2 and stretch

The test did find something, and the write-up put it down to chance

  • The redesigned form does not work on old mobile browsers. 379 of them used the current form in eight days; a roll-out would turn away about twice that.
  • On devices logged in both arms, completion is 61.0% on the current form and 59.3% on the redesign: the "right direction" disappears.
  • That second comparison is a repair made after the fact. It shows how fragile the claim is; it does not prove the reverse.

A reviewer's best contribution

Not the list of errors. The thing the data were saying that the author did not want to hear.

The 379 is the count in the current arm over eight days; about as many were assigned to the redesign and lost. This is the humanity principle in practice: an average that looks fine while one group of citizens cannot use the service at all. Say who would have been affected: people with older phones.
